How many investors should you contact?

Slavomír Hruška · 9 Nov 2026 · 8 min read
Short answer: There is no universal number. Build a sufficiently large, qualified investor list, then plan backward from the number of meetings and commitments you need using your own conversion assumptions. A smaller list of relevant investors can outperform a large list of funds that never invest at your stage.

Asking “How many VCs should I email?” is like asking how many sales calls make a customer. The count depends on fit, the strength of the proposition, the size of the ask and the path to a decision.

The funnel behind a fundraise

A useful operating model has five stages:

  1. Qualified targets: Funds that plausibly invest in your sector, geography, round stage and check size.
  2. First meetings: Investors who agree to evaluate the company.
  3. Serious follow-up: Second meetings, references, diligence questions or partner involvement.
  4. Term sheets or firm offers: Written financing terms requiring review.
  5. Closed commitments: Capital that actually completes and becomes available.

Not every investor follows exactly these steps. Angels may decide individually; institutional funds usually require more internal coordination. The model is for planning, not a universal description.

An illustrative example

Suppose you contact 120 qualified investors. Assume 25% take an initial meeting, 30% of those advance to substantive follow-up, 20% of follow-ups produce a term sheet, and 60% of term sheets close.

Funnel stageModeled result
Qualified investors120
First meetings30
Substantive follow-ups9
Term sheets1.8 expected
Closed investments1.08 expected

These are illustrative conversion assumptions, not measured market averages. Actual results are discrete and uncertain: you cannot receive 1.8 term sheets. The model simply tells you whether your plan is coherent.

Why lists fail even when they're long

Wrong stage

A fund that only leads Series A rounds isn't part of a relevant pre-seed pipeline, however impressive its logo.

Wrong check size

If you need a €1m lead, a fund that generally invests €50k cannot fill that role. Angels and followers may still be useful, but model them separately.

Weak connection between evidence and thesis

If every investor asks the same question about retention or distribution, sending another 100 emails does not fix the underlying issue. Revise the material or collect the missing evidence.

Poor process management

Founders sometimes lose momentum not because they lack leads, but because they fail to track introductions, open questions, promised documents and next steps.

Build a fundable investor list

Start with portfolio and thesis research. For each investor, record stage, sector, geography, typical check size, whether they lead, named decision-makers and a credible reason to approach. Then rank the list by fit and expected value, not prestige.

A practical set of columns is: Fund | Partner | Sector fit | Stage fit | Check size | Intro path | Contacted | Next action | Status | Confidence/concerns.

When to change the pitch rather than increase volume

If your first cohort produces no credible next steps, stop and diagnose. Was the outreach reaching the right people? Did investors understand the product in one sentence? Were repeated objections about the same missing evidence? Did the round size match the milestones?

Whatever the source, the logic holds: repeating an unsuccessful batch of outreach without adjusting the proposition is a poor use of time.

Frequently asked questions

Is cold outreach worth doing? It can be, if it's specific and credible. Research the fund first. A poor generic email to hundreds of investors is rarely efficient.

Do I need a lead investor? It depends on the instrument, stage and market. Priced institutional rounds typically benefit from a lead who sets terms, while many small pre-seed rounds may be assembled differently.

How many rejections are normal? Rejections are common, but a precise industry-wide rate would be misleading. Track your own results and ask whether repeated objections suggest a real pattern.
