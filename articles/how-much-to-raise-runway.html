How much should you raise? A runway-based approach

Slavomír Hruška · 9 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
Short answer: Calculate what it will cost to reach a meaningful milestone, subtract available cash and add an appropriate buffer. Then test whether the amount is financeable without giving up ownership or accepting terms you have not modeled. “Whatever the market offers” is not a financing plan.

Founders sometimes choose a round size because a peer raised that amount or because it sounds credible in a pitch. Investors can ask a much more useful question: what result will the money buy, and when will the company produce it?

Start with the milestone, not the valuation

Imagine a B2B software team that wants to prove repeatable customer acquisition. Its next milestone is not merely “grow fast”; it might be to reach a particular number of retained paying teams with documented acquisition costs. That requires staff, product work, sales activity and enough runway to see reliable results.

For a hardware company, the milestone might be certification, a commercial pilot or manufacturing readiness. The calendar and cost profile will differ considerably.

Write down the specific result, the evidence you will need and the date at which it should exist. Then cost the path backward.

A simple cash requirement formula

Target raise = max(0, monthly net burn × target months + one-time costs − cash on hand) × (1 + contingency rate)

Net burn is operating cash outflow minus recurring operating cash inflow, for this simplified model. Cash from new financing is intentionally excluded until it closes. This is a planning shortcut, not a complete cash-flow forecast.

Worked example

Suppose your company spends €45,000 per month, collects €10,000 per month, has €120,000 in cash, needs 20 months, expects a €40,000 one-time cost and chooses a 15% buffer.

  • Net monthly burn: €35,000.
  • Base cash requirement: €35,000 × 20 + €40,000 − €120,000 = €620,000.
  • With a 15% contingency: €713,000.

This calculation doesn't know whether your headcount or revenue will change. Build a monthly model before committing to a financing target.

Test the ownership impact

In a simplified priced equity round, a €713,000 investment at a €4 million pre-money valuation implies a post-money valuation of €4.713 million. The new investor's ownership would be roughly €713,000 / €4.713 million, or 15.1%, ignoring an option-pool increase, convertible securities, fees and other terms.

SAFEs work differently. A post-money SAFE's cap can make ownership sold easier to estimate, but the full cap table depends on the specific instrument, subsequent round and other securities. Do not apply the simple priced-equity formula to every SAFE or convertible note.

Don't confuse fundraising targets with runway targets

A founder might say “we want 24 months of runway.” That is a sensible planning horizon in some cases, not a universal law. If the next milestone takes six months, a huge round can be unnecessary. If the company needs regulatory approval or a long hardware cycle, 24 months may be insufficient.

Published guides on round sizing (see the Carta links below) can be a useful starting point, but benchmarks reflect the datasets and geographies they were built from. Do not import US medians uncritically into a CEE fundraise.

Model scenarios, not only the base case

At minimum, calculate:

  1. Base case: Your current best estimate of burn, growth and milestone date.
  2. Downside case: Slower sales, extra hiring or longer implementation.
  3. Constrained case: How you would adjust the plan if you raised less than the target.

The best answer to “Why this amount?” is a convincing narrative backed by a model and options—not the largest valuation you think you can negotiate.

Frequently asked questions

Should I raise more than I need? A cushion may reduce financing risk, but more equity funding can create dilution and expectations. Quantify both.

Does an investor's headline valuation tell me my dilution? Not by itself. You need to know whether it is pre- or post-money and what happens to option pools and convertible instruments.

Is this financial or legal advice? No. It's an educational framework, not a substitute for a detailed cash-flow model or qualified advisers.
