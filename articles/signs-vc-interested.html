Signs a VC is interested—or just being polite

Slavomír Hruška · 9 Nov 2026 · 8 min read
Short answer: Look for actions that cost the investor time or involve other decision-makers. A concrete diligence request or scheduled partner meeting is more informative than compliments. No individual signal guarantees investment.

A founder leaves the meeting energized. The investor smiled, asked questions, said the market was exciting and promised to stay in touch. Three weeks later, nothing happened. Was that real interest—or just a good conversation?

Fundraising gets easier when you separate how a meeting felt from what the investor did.

Stronger signs: the process is moving

They schedule an actual next step

A named date with a stated purpose—product demo, partner meeting, follow-up review—is stronger than “let's reconnect sometime.” It still may end in a pass, but at least the process is visible.

They bring in another decision-maker

Introducing a partner, principal or specialist can indicate that the opportunity is moving toward wider scrutiny. It is not necessarily endorsement: colleagues may be brought in to test specific concerns.

They ask for concrete evidence

Requests for cohort data, customer references, revenue detail or technical documentation show a willingness to evaluate risk. Confirm what is needed, in what format and when. Protect sensitive information and understand the recipient before sharing it.

They explain how the decision works

When an investor describes their investment committee, the likely questions and timing, founders can prepare a more effective next step. The information may also be routine, so interpret it in context.

Weaker signs: courtesy is easy

“Very exciting space,” “great founder,” “love the idea,” and “keep us in the loop” may all be sincere while indicating nothing about the investment decision. They require little effort to say.

A warm tone is not a term sheet. Nor is a cold tone necessarily a rejection: some engaged investors are challenging because their job is to test the thesis.

Four combinations to recognize

BehaviorSensible interpretationNext action
Compliments + no actionPleasant but unqualifiedAsk one clear process question
Hard questions + requested dataActive evaluation is plausibleAnswer accurately and confirm next step
Enthusiasm + repeated missed deadlinesMomentum is uncertainClarify the timeline; keep the pipeline moving
Explicit fund mismatchA likely pass despite warmthSeek a more suitable investor

Ask this at the end of the meeting

“Based on what we discussed, is this within your current investment criteria? If yes, what specific decision would come next, and what would help you make it?”

This invites clarity without asking for an immediate term sheet. If the answer remains vague, record it as uncertainty rather than silently upgrading your forecast.

Avoid turning qualitative signals into fake probabilities

No checklist can tell you that an investor is “83% likely” to write a check without a properly calibrated dataset. Even data-driven scores are sensitive to geography, funding stage, partner, mandate and market conditions.

Treat a decoder as a conversation preparation aid, not a probability model. Tracking your own actual process conversions over time will be more useful than assigning arbitrary conversion rates to someone else's smile.

Frequently asked questions

Does a second meeting mean I'm close to a term sheet? No. It means the investor wants another conversation. The remaining steps may still be substantial.

Is a request for a data room a strong signal? It shows evaluation interest, but funds differ in how routinely they request documents. Ask what decision the materials will inform.

Can I ask the investor to be more direct? Yes. A clear process question is usually more productive than trying to decode body language.
